## Phase 0: Prerequisites & Orientation
> Before learning what to eat, learn how to look at food.

### Module 0.1 — Your Body Needs Stuff
> What even is a nutrient? Why does food matter beyond "I'm hungry"?

- [ ] What food does for your body: energy, building material, regulation
- [ ] The three **macronutrients**: protein, carbohydrates, fat — a first look (detailed breakdown in Phase 1)
- [ ] The concept of **micronutrients**: vitamins and minerals — small amounts, big importance
- [ ] **Calories** — what they are, why they're not the whole story
- [ ] Context connection: "When you feel tired at 3pm or wake up groggy — that's your body telling you something about what you ate."
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** Without looking anything up, write down what you think you ate yesterday. Guess which foods gave you protein, which gave you carbs, which gave you fat. We'll check your guesses later.
- **Exercise (Kitchen):** Pick 5 things from your fridge or pantry. Read the packaging. For each one, write down: calories per serving, grams of protein, carbs, and fat. What patterns do you notice?

### Module 0.2 — Reading Food Labels
> The nutrition label is a cheat sheet — but only if you know how to read it.
> *Stanford "Introduction to Food and Health" Wk3: label reading & grocery shopping*

- [ ] Anatomy of a nutrition label: serving size, calories, macros, % daily value
- [ ] **Serving size** vs how much you actually eat — the most common trap
- [ ] The **ingredients list** — what order means, names to recognize, names that are just sugar in disguise
- [ ] **% Daily Value** — what it means, when it's useful, when it's misleading
- [ ] "Natural," "organic," "whole grain," "plant-based" — what these labels actually guarantee (and don't)
- [ ] Context connection: "You've seen '0g trans fat' on labels of things that definitely have trans fat. Let's learn why that's legal."
- **Exercise (Kitchen):** Pick 3 packaged foods you eat regularly. For each: what's the actual serving size vs how much you eat? Recalculate the real nutrient values for your actual portion.
- **Exercise (Grocery):** Next time you're at the store, compare two brands of the same product (e.g., two yogurts, two breads). Which is more nutritious and why? Write a 3-sentence verdict.
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** A cereal box says "Good source of iron — 25% DV." The serving size is 30g (about 3/4 cup). You pour yourself a big bowl, probably 60g. How much iron are you actually getting relative to the DV? Is this a good thing or does it not matter?

## Phase 1: Foundations — What Your Body Needs
> The science behind food. After this phase, you'll understand what nutrients are, what they do, and where vegetarians get them.
> *Aligns with: Stanford Wk1 (nutrients & functions), Precision Nutrition L1 Unit 1 (Ch4-8), Wageningen Macronutrients (Mod1-4)*

### Module 1.1 — Macronutrients: The Big Three
> Protein, carbs, and fat — what they are, what they do, and how much you need.
> *Wageningen "Macronutrients and Overnutrition" Mod2-4, Precision Nutrition L1 Ch6*

- [ ] **Protein**: amino acids, complete vs incomplete proteins, how much you actually need (RDA: 0.8g/kg, but context matters)
- [ ] **Carbohydrates**: simple vs complex, fiber, glycemic index — why not all carbs are equal
- [ ] **Fat**: saturated, unsaturated, trans — which matter and why. Omega-3 and omega-6 balance.
- [ ] **Calories in vs calories out** — the energy balance concept and why it's true but incomplete
- [ ] How macros work together in a meal — why you feel different after rice alone vs rice with tofu and vegetables
- [ ] Vegetarian protein sources ranked: legumes, tofu, tempeh, seitan, eggs, dairy, quinoa, nuts/seeds
- [ ] Context connection: "That crash after eating a big bowl of white rice? That's a carb-heavy meal with no protein or fat to slow absorption. Let's understand why."
- **Exercise (Spreadsheet):** Build a "My Protein Sources" table. List 15 vegetarian protein sources with: food, serving size, protein per serving, calories per serving, whether it's a complete protein. Sort by protein-per-calorie ratio. Which sources give you the most protein for the fewest calories?
- **Exercise (Kitchen):** Cook two versions of the same meal: one high-carb/low-protein (e.g., pasta with tomato sauce), one balanced (e.g., pasta with lentil bolognese). Eat them on separate days. Note how you feel 1 hour and 3 hours after each. Write down what you notice.
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** "Alex eats: oatmeal with banana for breakfast, a cheese sandwich for lunch, pasta with tomato sauce for dinner. Roughly estimate his macro split for the day (what % protein, carbs, fat). Where are the gaps?"
- **Quiz:** 10 questions. Identify which macronutrient is the main energy source in common foods. True/false on protein myths. Match foods to whether they're complete or incomplete proteins. "Which meal would keep you fullest longest and why?"

### Module 1.2 — Whole Foods vs Processed Foods
> Not all food is created equal — even if the nutrition label looks similar.
> *Stanford "Introduction to Food and Health" Wk2: processed vs whole food*

- [ ] What **food processing** actually means — a spectrum from "washed and cut" to "manufactured in a factory"
- [ ] The **NOVA classification**: unprocessed, processed ingredients, processed foods, ultra-processed foods
- [ ] Why ultra-processed foods are a problem — it's not just the nutrients, it's what processing does to how you eat
- [ ] **Nutrient density** — calories that come with nutrients vs calories that come empty
- [ ] Whole grains vs refined grains — what's actually removed and why it matters
- [ ] Vegetarian trap: many meat substitutes are ultra-processed. How to evaluate them.
- [ ] Context connection: "That veggie burger patty might have more ingredients than you'd expect. Let's learn when that matters and when it doesn't."
- **Exercise (Kitchen):** Check 5 items in your kitchen. Classify each as NOVA 1 (unprocessed), 2 (processed ingredient), 3 (processed), or 4 (ultra-processed). What's your ratio?
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** "Compare: plain Greek yogurt with berries vs a flavored yogurt with 'real fruit.' Both say 'good source of protein.' What's the actual difference?"
- **Exercise (Grocery):** Find a vegetarian meat substitute you've bought before. Read the ingredients. Count the total ingredients. How many can you recognize as actual foods? Research what the unrecognizable ones are.

### Module 1.3 — How Your Body Uses Food
> From mouth to cell — a simplified tour of digestion.
> *Precision Nutrition L1 Ch2: digestion & absorption, Wageningen Mod1*

- [ ] The digestive system in plain English: mouth → stomach → small intestine → large intestine
- [ ] **Enzymes** — the workers that break food into usable pieces
- [ ] How proteins, carbs, and fats are broken down differently
- [ ] **Absorption** — where nutrients actually enter your body (mostly the small intestine)
- [ ] **Fiber** and the large intestine — why fiber is special (your body can't digest it, but your gut bacteria can)
- [ ] Why food combining is mostly a myth — your body handles mixed meals just fine
- [ ] Context connection: "Ever wondered why beans make you gassy? It's because of specific carbohydrates that your enzymes can't break down, so your gut bacteria do it instead — and they produce gas."
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** Trace a meal through the digestive system. "You eat a bowl of lentil soup with whole grain bread. What happens to the protein in the lentils? The starch in the bread? The fat from the olive oil drizzle?"
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** "Your friend says you shouldn't eat fruit after a meal because it 'ferments in your stomach.' Using what you know about digestion, explain why this is wrong."

### Module 1.4 — Micronutrients: Vitamins and Minerals
> The small stuff that does big work.
> *Wageningen "Micronutrients and Malnutrition" full course, Precision Nutrition L1 Ch7*

- [ ] **Vitamins**: fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) vs water-soluble (B vitamins, C) — why the distinction matters
- [ ] **Minerals**: major minerals (calcium, magnesium, potassium) vs trace minerals (iron, zinc, iodine, selenium)
- [ ] What each key vitamin and mineral does — a practical reference, not memorization
- [ ] **Bioavailability** — why the amount in food isn't the same as the amount your body absorbs
- [ ] Enhancers and inhibitors: vitamin C boosts iron absorption, phytates reduce zinc absorption
- [ ] The vegetarian watchlist preview: B12, iron, zinc, calcium, vitamin D, omega-3, iodine (detailed in Phase 2)
- [ ] Context connection: "You could eat plenty of iron-rich spinach, but without vitamin C in the same meal, your body absorbs much less of it. Pairing matters."
- **Exercise (Spreadsheet):** Build a "Vegetarian Micronutrient Map." For each of the 7 watchlist nutrients: what it does, RDA, top 5 vegetarian food sources with amounts per serving, absorption tips.
- **Exercise (Kitchen):** Plan one day of meals specifically to maximize iron absorption. Include vitamin C sources with each iron-rich food. Avoid tea/coffee within 1 hour of iron-rich meals. Write down what you'd eat.
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** "Alex takes a multivitamin every morning with his coffee. Is this a good idea? What would you change and why?"
- **Quiz:** 12 questions. Match vitamins to their function. Identify which foods are rich in which minerals. True/false on absorption enhancers and inhibitors. "Which of these meal combinations gives you the most absorbable iron?"

### Module 1.5 — Water and Hydration
> The nutrient everyone forgets.
> *Precision Nutrition L1 Ch8: water & fluid balance*

- [ ] Why water matters — every chemical reaction in your body needs it
- [ ] How much water you actually need (spoiler: "8 glasses a day" is not based on much)
- [ ] **Hydration signals** — thirst, urine color, energy levels
- [ ] Water from food — fruits, vegetables, soups all count
- [ ] Caffeine and hydration — the truth (it's milder than you think)
- [ ] Context connection: "That headache you get on days you forget to drink water? That's your brain literally shrinking slightly from dehydration. Let's understand why."
- **Exercise (Kitchen):** Track your water intake for 3 days (include water from food and beverages). Note energy levels and urine color. Do you see a pattern?
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** "Is herbal tea as hydrating as water? What about milk? Orange juice? Rank these beverages by hydration effectiveness and explain your reasoning."

## Phase 2: Vegetarian Practice — Making It Work
> Now you know what nutrients are. This phase is about making sure YOU get enough of them.
> *Aligns with: "Becoming Vegan" (Davis & Melina) chapters on protein, minerals, vitamins, fatty acids. Precision Nutrition L1 Ch9 (special needs) and Ch14 (supplementation). Mayo Clinic vegetarian nutrition guide.*

### Module 2.1 — Protein Without Meat
> The question every vegetarian gets asked. After this module, you'll have a real answer.
> *"Becoming Vegan" Ch3: protein & amino acids. Precision Nutrition L1 Ch6: macronutrients.*

- [ ] **Complete vs incomplete proteins** — why it matters less than people think
- [ ] The 9 **essential amino acids** — what "essential" means and which plant foods have which
- [ ] **Protein combining** — the outdated rule and the modern understanding (you don't need to combine at every meal, just over the course of a day)
- [ ] How much protein do you actually need? RDA (0.8g/kg) vs evidence for higher intake
- [ ] **Leucine** — the amino acid that matters most for muscle maintenance, and where vegetarians find it
- [ ] The vegetarian protein tier list:
  - Tier 1 (complete, high-density): eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, tofu, tempeh, edamame
  - Tier 2 (complete or near-complete): quinoa, buckwheat, soy milk, seitan
  - Tier 3 (incomplete but valuable): lentils, chickpeas, black beans, peanut butter, oats, rice
  - Tier 4 (supplementary): nuts, seeds, whole grains, vegetables
- [ ] Context connection: "You've probably heard 'beans and rice make a complete protein.' That's true, but you don't even need to eat them in the same meal. Your body is smarter than that."
- **Exercise (Spreadsheet):** Using your "My Protein Sources" table from Module 1.1, plan 3 days of meals where you hit at least 60g of protein per day (adjust for your weight if you know it — 0.8g/kg minimum). Every meal should have at least one Tier 1 or Tier 2 source. Calculate your daily totals.
- **Exercise (Kitchen):** Cook a meal using a protein source you've never tried: tempeh, seitan, or edamame. Rate it honestly — would you eat this regularly?
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** "A friend is vegetarian and eats cereal for breakfast, a cheese pizza for lunch, and pasta with garlic bread for dinner. They say they're fine because cheese has protein. What's missing and what would you suggest?"
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** "Why is tofu a Tier 1 protein but peanut butter is Tier 3, even though a big spoonful of peanut butter has decent protein? Think about protein density, amino acid profile, and what else comes along for the ride."
- **Quiz:** 10 questions. Rank protein sources by quality. True/false on combining myths. Calculate daily protein needs for a given body weight. "Design a day of vegetarian eating that hits 70g protein."

### Module 2.2 — The Vegetarian Nutrient Watchlist
> Six nutrients that need your attention. Not because vegetarian diets are bad — but because you need to be intentional.
> *"Becoming Vegan" Ch5-11 (individual nutrients). Mayo Clinic vegetarian diet guide. Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020-2025 (RDAs). Examine.com (supplement evidence).*

- [ ] **Vitamin B12** — the non-negotiable supplement. Can't get it reliably from plant foods. Period. (RDA: 2.4μg/day)
  - Sources: fortified foods (nutritional yeast, plant milks, cereals), eggs, dairy, or a supplement
  - Why it matters: nerve function, DNA synthesis, red blood cells
  - Deficiency is slow and sneaky — symptoms can take years to appear
- [ ] **Iron** — you can get enough, but you need to be strategic. (RDA: 18mg/day for vegetarians — 1.8x the standard RDA because plant iron is less absorbable)
  - **Heme vs non-heme iron**: meat has both, plants have only non-heme (less absorbed)
  - Absorption boosters: vitamin C, cooking in cast iron
  - Absorption blockers: tea, coffee, calcium — don't combine with iron-rich meals
  - Best vegetarian sources: lentils, chickpeas, fortified cereals, spinach (cooked), tofu, pumpkin seeds
- [ ] **Zinc** — commonly low in vegetarian diets and often overlooked. (RDA: 11mg men, 8mg women)
  - Plant zinc is less bioavailable due to **phytates** in legumes and grains
  - Soaking, sprouting, and fermenting reduce phytates
  - Best sources: pumpkin seeds, chickpeas, lentils, cashews, oats, cheese, yogurt
- [ ] **Calcium** — not just dairy. (RDA: 1000mg/day)
  - If you eat dairy, you're probably fine. If you limit dairy, plan for it.
  - Non-dairy sources: fortified plant milks, tofu (calcium-set), kale, broccoli, almonds, figs
  - **Oxalates** — spinach has calcium but oxalates block absorption. Kale and broccoli are better.
- [ ] **Vitamin D** — the "sunshine vitamin" that most people (not just vegetarians) are low in. (RDA: 600-800 IU/day)
  - Sun exposure is the primary source. Food sources are limited.
  - Vegetarian food sources: fortified milks, egg yolks, mushrooms (UV-exposed)
  - Supplementation is common and reasonable — D3 from lichen is vegetarian
- [ ] **Omega-3 fatty acids** — the hardest one to get right without fish. (No official RDA, but 250-500mg EPA+DHA recommended)
  - **ALA** (plant-based): flaxseed, chia seeds, walnuts, hemp seeds — your body converts some ALA to EPA/DHA, but poorly (5-10%)
  - **EPA and DHA** (what your body actually needs): normally from fish. Vegetarian option: algae-based supplement.
  - Context connection: "This is the one where you probably need a supplement if you're not eating fish. ALA from flax is great, but your body isn't very efficient at converting it."
- **Exercise (Spreadsheet):** Build your "Watchlist Tracker." For each of the 6 nutrients: your RDA, your top 5 food sources with amounts per serving, and whether you need a supplement. Highlight any where you're likely falling short based on what you currently eat.
- **Exercise (Kitchen):** Plan a "nutrient-dense day" — one full day of meals where you consciously hit your targets for B12, iron, calcium, and protein. Cook at least one of the meals.
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** "Alex drinks coffee with breakfast (oatmeal with fortified soy milk) and tea with lunch (lentil soup). What nutrient absorption problems might this cause? What would you change?"
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** "Rank these supplements from 'definitely take this' to 'probably don't need it' for a lacto-ovo vegetarian: B12, iron, vitamin D, omega-3 (algae), zinc, calcium. Explain your ranking."
- **Quiz:** 12 questions. Match nutrients to deficiency symptoms. Identify absorption boosters and blockers. "This person eats X — which nutrients are they likely low in?" MCQ on supplement necessity.

### Module 2.3 — Meal Planning for Vegetarians
> Putting it all together — building days and weeks of eating that actually cover your bases.
> *Precision Nutrition L1 Ch13: providing a nutrition plan. "Becoming Vegan": vegan plate model and sample menus.*

- [ ] The **vegetarian plate model** — a visual framework for balanced meals:
  - 1/4 plate protein (legumes, tofu, tempeh, eggs, dairy)
  - 1/4 plate whole grains or starchy vegetables
  - 1/2 plate vegetables (variety of colors)
  - Plus: a source of healthy fat (nuts, seeds, olive oil, avocado)
- [ ] Building a **weekly rotation** — variety prevents deficiency and boredom
- [ ] **Batch cooking** basics — cook once, eat several times. Which vegetarian proteins and grains hold up well?
- [ ] Meal planning on a budget — the most nutritious cheap vegetarian foods (lentils, eggs, frozen vegetables, oats, beans)
- [ ] Eating out as a vegetarian — how to make decent choices without overthinking
- [ ] Context connection: "You don't need to plan every meal perfectly. If most of your meals follow the plate model, the occasional bowl of cereal for dinner is fine."
- **Exercise (Spreadsheet):** Build a 5-day meal plan using the plate model. For each day, calculate total protein, iron, and calcium. Aim for variety — don't repeat the same protein source on consecutive days.
- **Exercise (Kitchen):** Pick one day from your meal plan and actually cook it. All three meals. Take notes on what worked and what was annoying.
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** "You're going to a restaurant with friends. The menu has: a veggie burger, a margherita pizza, a falafel wrap, a Caesar salad with croutons (no chicken), and pasta primavera. Rank them from most to least nutritionally complete. What would you add to the weakest option to balance it?"

### Module 2.4 — Supplementation: What You Actually Need
> The honest guide — some supplements are essential, most are marketing.
> *Precision Nutrition L1 Ch14: supplementation. Examine.com evidence reviews.*

- [ ] The hierarchy: **food first, then supplement the gaps**
- [ ] **Essential for most vegetarians:**
  - Vitamin B12 (no reliable plant source)
  - Vitamin D (especially if you don't get much sun)
- [ ] **Consider based on your diet:**
  - Algae-based omega-3 (EPA/DHA) — if you don't eat fish (you don't)
  - Iron — only if blood work shows you're low (don't supplement iron without testing)
- [ ] **Probably unnecessary if you eat well:**
  - Calcium — if you eat dairy or fortified foods regularly
  - Zinc — if you eat legumes and whole grains regularly
  - Multivitamins — usually redundant if you're covering the essentials
- [ ] How to read a supplement label — doses, forms, third-party testing (USP, NSF)
- [ ] The supplement industry — what's regulated and what's not
- [ ] Context connection: "B12 and D are the 'just take them' supplements. Everything else depends on how you eat. Blood work is the real answer."
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** "Alex's friend takes: a multivitamin, vitamin C, B-complex, calcium, iron, fish oil (but he's vegetarian?), and turmeric capsules. Which ones are useful, which are redundant, which might be harmful, and which is just wrong?"
- **Exercise (Spreadsheet):** Build your personal supplement plan. For each supplement: which one, what form, what dose, when to take it, and why. Base it on what you learned about your own gaps in Module 2.2.
- **Exercise (Grocery):** Go to a supplement aisle (or website). Find a B12 supplement, a vitamin D supplement, and an algae-based omega-3. Compare 3 brands of each: price per dose, form, third-party testing. Which would you buy?

## Phase 3: Going Deeper — Sustainability and Critical Thinking
> You know what to eat. Now learn how to keep doing it and how to evaluate the endless nutrition advice you'll encounter.
> *Aligns with: NASM CNC Ch14 (psychology of behavior change), Ch22 (nutrition hot topics). Stanford Wk2 (Michael Pollan, food philosophy). Wageningen Mod1 (study designs).*

### Module 3.1 — Nutrition Myths vs Evidence
> How to tell real nutrition science from wellness influencer nonsense.
> *NASM CNC Ch22: nutrition hot topics & controversies. Stanford: evaluating food claims. Wageningen Mod1: study designs in nutrition research.*

- [ ] Why nutrition science is hard — confounding variables, correlation vs causation, self-reported data
- [ ] **Study types** ranked by strength: anecdote < case study < observational < randomized controlled trial < meta-analysis
- [ ] How to read a nutrition headline: "Coffee causes cancer!" vs what the study actually said
- [ ] Common vegetarian myths debunked with evidence:
  - "Soy gives you estrogen problems" — what the research actually shows
  - "You can't build muscle without meat" — evidence from protein studies
  - "Vegetarian diets are automatically healthy" — nope, Oreos are vegetarian
  - "You need to combine proteins at every meal" — outdated since the 1970s
- [ ] **Red flags** in nutrition advice: selling supplements, before/after photos, "one weird trick," citing single studies
- [ ] Context connection: "Next time someone tells you something about nutrition, ask: 'What kind of study showed that?' You'll filter out 90% of nonsense."
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** Find a nutrition headline from this week (news, social media, anywhere). What kind of study is it based on? What are the limitations? Would you change your diet based on it?
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** "A viral post claims eating soy increases estrogen and is dangerous for men. Write a 5-sentence response using what you know about evidence levels and what the research actually shows."
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** "Rank these sources of nutrition information from most to least trustworthy: your doctor, a fitness influencer with 500K followers, a meta-analysis in a peer-reviewed journal, your grandmother, a Netflix documentary, the Mayo Clinic website. Explain your ranking."

### Module 3.2 — Building Sustainable Habits
> The best diet is the one you actually follow.
> *Precision Nutrition L1 Ch15: behavior goals & monitoring. NASM CNC Ch14: psychology of behavior change. Harvard "6-Week Plan for Healthy Eating."*

- [ ] Why motivation fades and **systems** outlast willpower
- [ ] **Habit stacking** — attaching new food habits to existing routines
- [ ] **The 80/20 approach** — eat well most of the time, don't stress the rest
- [ ] Dealing with **decision fatigue** — meal prep, go-to meals, default choices
- [ ] Common failure modes for vegetarians: boredom (eating the same 5 things), convenience traps (defaulting to cheese and bread), social pressure
- [ ] Context connection: "You don't need to eat perfectly. You need to eat well enough, consistently, without it being a source of stress."
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** Identify your top 3 "failure modes" — situations where you eat poorly. For each, design one small system to make the better choice easier (not harder to make the bad choice — easier to make the good one).
- **Exercise (Kitchen):** Build your "default meal" list — 5 meals you can make in under 20 minutes with ingredients you always have. These are your "I don't feel like cooking" safety net.
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** "Alex wants to eat more legumes but keeps forgetting to soak dried beans. What are 3 practical solutions that don't require more willpower?"

### Module 3.3 — Gut Health and Digestion
> What your gut bacteria do for you and how to feed them well.
> *Precision Nutrition L1 Ch2: GI tract. Wageningen "Human Microbiome" course. "Becoming Vegan" Ch2.*

- [ ] The **gut microbiome** — trillions of bacteria that affect digestion, immunity, and even mood
- [ ] **Prebiotics** — the fiber that feeds good bacteria (onions, garlic, bananas, oats, legumes)
- [ ] **Probiotics** — live bacteria in food (yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi, miso, tempeh)
- [ ] Why vegetarians often have healthier microbiomes — high fiber diets feed diverse bacteria
- [ ] **Bloating and gas** from high-fiber/legume-heavy diets — why it happens and how to manage the transition
- [ ] Food intolerances vs allergies — different mechanisms, different severity
- [ ] Context connection: "If you've increased your bean intake and feel gassy — that's actually a sign your gut bacteria are adjusting. It usually calms down in 2-3 weeks."
- **Exercise (Kitchen):** Add one fermented food to your diet daily for a week (yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi, miso soup, tempeh). Note any changes in digestion.
- **Exercise (Conceptual):** "Design a one-day meal plan that maximizes gut health: include prebiotic foods, probiotic foods, and plenty of fiber variety. Explain why each choice supports the microbiome."
